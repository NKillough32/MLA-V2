### Q1 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Emergency Medicine

A 35-year-old male presents to the emergency department with signs of anaphylaxis after eating peanuts. He is experiencing difficulty breathing, widespread urticaria, and hypotension. He is known to be allergic to peanuts, and his observations include a blood pressure of 80/40 mmHg and oxygen saturation of 88% on room air.

**Complete a prescription for the most appropriate immediate treatment.**

DRUG: Adrenaline (epinephrine) | Adrenaline | Epinephrine  
DOSE: 500 micrograms | 0.5 mg | 500 mcg  
ROUTE: Intramuscular (IM) | IM  
FREQUENCY: As required, every 5 minutes | PRN, every 5 minutes | PRN, q5min  
INDICATION: Anaphylaxis

> Adrenaline is the first-line treatment for anaphylaxis due to its rapid action in reversing hypotension and airway constriction. The intramuscular route is preferred in emergency settings for fast absorption and ease of administration. Doses can be repeated every 5 minutes if the patient does not improve. Alternatives like chlorphenamine or hydrocortisone are not immediate treatments and do not address the acute cardiovascular and respiratory symptoms. Incorrect doses or delayed adrenaline can lead to worsening anaphylaxis and potential cardiac arrest.

### Q2 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Infectious Diseases

A 67-year-old woman is admitted with suspected sepsis secondary to a urinary tract infection. Her past medical history includes hypertension and type 2 diabetes. She is febrile, tachycardic, and hypotensive. Blood cultures are pending.

**Complete a prescription for the most appropriate empirical antibiotic treatment.**

DRUG: Piperacillin-tazobactam | Tazocin  
DOSE: 4.5 grams | 4500 mg | 4,500 mg  
ROUTE: Intravenous (IV) | IV  
FREQUENCY: Every 8 hours | TDS | q8h  
INDICATION: Empirical treatment of sepsis

> Piperacillin-tazobactam is a broad-spectrum antibiotic effective against common organisms causing sepsis, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Enterobacteriaceae. It is indicated for empirical treatment pending culture results. The IV route ensures rapid achievement of therapeutic concentrations. Alternatives such as amoxicillin may not cover resistant organisms adequately. Delaying or choosing incorrect antibiotics can result in progression to septic shock.

### Q3 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Respiratory Medicine

A 25-year-old female with a history of asthma presents to A&E with acute shortness of breath, wheezing, and a peak flow at 60% of her best. Her respiratory rate is 28 breaths per minute and oxygen saturation is 92% on air.

**Complete a prescription for the most appropriate immediate treatment.**

DRUG: Salbutamol | Ventolin  
DOSE: 2.5 mg  
ROUTE: Nebulised | Neb  
FREQUENCY: Every 20 minutes, as needed | q20min PRN | PRN, q20min  
INDICATION: Acute asthma exacerbation

> Salbutamol is a first-line bronchodilator in acute asthma to relieve bronchospasm. Nebulisation provides rapid delivery to the lungs, which is crucial in an acute setting. Frequent dosing (every 20 minutes) is recommended until symptoms improve. Other options like ipratropium or systemic corticosteroids are supplementary and not immediately effective as salbutamol. Delaying treatment can lead to respiratory failure.

### Q4 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Cardiology

A 68-year-old male with a history of hypertension and type 2 diabetes presents with sudden onset central chest pain radiating to his left arm. He is diaphoretic and pale, with BP 140/90 mmHg and HR 95 bpm. An ECG shows ST elevation in leads II, III, and aVF.

**Complete a prescription for the most appropriate immediate treatment.**

DRUG: Aspirin
DOSE: 300 mg  
ROUTE: Oral (PO) | PO  
FREQUENCY: Stat | STAT  
INDICATION: Acute myocardial infarction

> Aspirin is essential in acute myocardial infarction for its antiplatelet effect, reducing mortality by preventing thrombus progression. The stat dose ensures rapid platelet inhibition. Other treatments, such as clopidogrel or ticagrelor, may be added but do not replace initial aspirin therapy. Omitting or delaying aspirin can increase infarct size and worsen clinical outcomes.

### Q5 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Endocrinology

A 23-year-old female with type 1 diabetes is admitted with diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). She has nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain. Her blood glucose is 28 mmol/L, and blood gases show a metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.1.

**Complete a prescription for the most appropriate immediate treatment.**

DRUG: Insulin (soluble) | Actrapid  
DOSE: 0.1 units/kg/hour  
ROUTE: Intravenous infusion (IV) | IV Infusion  
FREQUENCY: Continuous  
INDICATION: Diabetic ketoacidosis

> Intravenous insulin infusion is crucial to manage DKA by reducing blood glucose and ketone levels. The dose is weight-based to ensure adequate treatment while avoiding hypoglycaemia. Subcutaneous insulin is inappropriate due to delayed action. Correcting DKA promptly prevents complications such as cerebral oedema. Other interventions, such as fluids and electrolytes, are equally important but do not replace insulin therapy.

### Q6 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Urology

A 28-year-old female presents to her GP with dysuria, frequency, and urgency. She has no fever or flank pain and is otherwise healthy. A mid-stream urine sample is sent for culture.

**Complete a prescription for the most appropriate first-line treatment.**

DRUG: Nitrofurantoin | Macrobid  
DOSE: 100 mg modified-release  
ROUTE: Oral (PO) | PO  
FREQUENCY: Twice daily (BD) | BD  
DURATION: 3 days  
INDICATION: Uncomplicated urinary tract infection

> Nitrofurantoin modified-release 100 mg BD for 3 days is the NICE/BNF first-line treatment for uncomplicated lower UTIs in non-pregnant women, chosen for its efficacy against E. coli and high urinary concentrations. A 3-day course balances effectiveness with tolerability and is preferred over longer courses in current guidance. Trimethoprim is an alternative but local resistance prevalence should be checked. Pivmecillinam is also a NICE-recommended first-line option.

### Q7 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Gastroenterology

A 56-year-old male is admitted with diarrhoea and abdominal pain following recent antibiotic use for pneumonia. Clostridium difficile toxin assay is positive.

**Complete a prescription for the most appropriate immediate treatment.**

DRUG: Vancomycin  
DOSE: 125 mg  
ROUTE: Oral (PO) | PO  
FREQUENCY: Four times daily (QDS) | QDS  
DURATION: 10 days  
INDICATION: Clostridioides difficile infection

> Oral vancomycin 125 mg QDS for 10 days is NICE first-line treatment for C. difficile infection (CDI), acting locally in the gut with minimal systemic absorption. Fidaxomicin is now recommended as the second-line agent (not metronidazole) for recurrent or refractory CDI when vancomycin has failed, owing to its superior sustained clinical response. Metronidazole is no longer recommended routinely for CDI by NICE. Prompt treatment is essential to prevent complications such as toxic megacolon or bowel perforation.

### Q8 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Neurology

A 45-year-old male presents with a severe, unilateral headache, centered around the left eye, associated with tearing and nasal congestion. He has had similar episodes in the past, and describes this as typical of his cluster headaches.

**Complete a prescription for the most appropriate immediate treatment.**

DRUG: Sumatriptan | Imigran  
DOSE: 6 mg  
ROUTE: Subcutaneous (SC) | SC  
FREQUENCY: Stat | STAT  
INDICATION: Acute cluster headache

> Subcutaneous sumatriptan 6 mg is one of two first-line treatments for acute cluster headache; the other is high-flow 100% oxygen at 10–15 L/min via a non-rebreather mask for 15–20 minutes. Both are recommended by BASH/NICE as first-line options. Oral triptans are ineffective due to their slow onset relative to the rapid peak of a cluster attack. IV verapamil is not used acutely. Failure to treat promptly can result in prolonged, severe pain.

### Q14 | REVIEW | Prescription Review | Gastroenterology

MARKS_B: 2

A 70-year-old male with a history of atrial fibrillation presents with gastrointestinal bleeding. He is currently on anticoagulation with warfarin.

**Current prescription:**
| Drug       | Dose | Route | Frequency | Indication         |
|------------|------|-------|-----------|--------------------|
| Warfarin   | 5 mg | Oral  | OD        | Atrial Fibrillation |
| Omeprazole | 20 mg | Oral  | OD        | Gastroprotection   |

**Part A: What is the problem with the current prescription?**

A. Omeprazole is ineffective for gastroprotection.
B. Warfarin increases the risk of bleeding. ✓
C. Omeprazole dose is too low.
D. Warfarin should be given BD.
E. Omeprazole should be given BD.

### Q18 | MCQ | Planning Management | Cardiology

A 60-year-old female with a history of hypertension and type 2 diabetes presents with chest pain and is diagnosed with a non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). She is already on aspirin 75 mg OD and atorvastatin 40 mg OD.

**What medication should be added to her treatment regimen?**

A. Ramipril 5 mg OD
B. Ticagrelor 180 mg loading dose, then 90 mg BD ✓
C. Clopidogrel 75 mg OD
D. Fondaparinux 2.5 mg SC OD
E. Metoprolol 25 mg BD

> NICE guidance (NG185) recommends dual antiplatelet therapy for NSTEMI. Since this patient is already on aspirin 75 mg OD, the key addition is a P2Y12 inhibitor. Ticagrelor (180 mg loading dose, then 90 mg BD) is the preferred first-line agent in most NSTEMI patients without contraindications, offering faster and more consistent platelet inhibition than clopidogrel. Clopidogrel 75 mg OD is an alternative if ticagrelor is not tolerated or contraindicated. Fondaparinux 2.5 mg SC OD is the preferred anticoagulant in NSTEMI managed conservatively but is not the primary answer here. Beta-blockers (e.g. metoprolol) and ACE inhibitors (e.g. ramipril) are important later in NSTEMI management but are not the immediate priority when antiplatelet therapy has not yet been optimised.

### Q27 | MCQ | Providing Prescribing Decisions | Cardiology

A 74-year-old female with hypertension and heart failure has been prescribed lisinopril. She reports experiencing a persistent dry cough.

**What advice should you provide regarding her current medication?**

A. Continue lisinopril, as the cough will resolve
B. Switch to an angiotensin II receptor blocker (ARB) ✓
