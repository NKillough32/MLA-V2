### Q1 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Emergency Medicine

A 45-year-old male presents to the A&E with acute shortness of breath, wheezing, and a history of asthma. His respiratory rate is 28/min, and he has an oxygen saturation of 90% on room air. He is using accessory muscles to breathe, and his peak flow is 150 L/min. 

**Complete a prescription for the most appropriate immediate treatment.**

DRUG: Salbutamol | Ventolin
DOSE: 5 mg | 5000 micrograms | 5,000 mcg
ROUTE: Nebulised (NEB) | Neb | NEB
FREQUENCY: Immediately; repeat every 20-30 minutes if inadequate response | q20-30min
INDICATION: Acute severe exacerbation of asthma

> Salbutamol 5 mg nebulised is the first-line bronchodilator in acute severe asthma. Delivery should be via an oxygen-driven nebuliser (6-8 L/min), both to correct hypoxaemia and to drive the aerosol. In severe or life-threatening attacks, doses can be repeated every 20-30 minutes, or given continuously. Prescribing as "PRN" alone is insufficient for an acute severe asthma scenario; the initial dose should be given immediately and repeat doses specified. Oral prednisolone 40-50 mg (or IV hydrocortisone) and ipratropium bromide are given alongside but do not replace the initial bronchodilator.

### Q2 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Infectious Diseases

An 82-year-old female with a history of COPD is admitted from a care home with fever, productive cough, and confusion consistent with healthcare-associated pneumonia and sepsis. Blood pressure is 95/60 mmHg, heart rate 110 bpm, and temperature 38.5°C. She has no known drug allergies. Blood cultures are taken and she requires empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy.

**Complete a prescription for the most appropriate immediate treatment.**

DRUG: Piperacillin-tazobactam | Tazocin
DOSE: 4.5 g | 4500 mg | 4,500 mg
ROUTE: Intravenous (IV) | IV | Intravenously
FREQUENCY: Every 8 hours (TDS) | TDS | Three times daily
INDICATION: Empirical treatment of healthcare-associated pneumonia and sepsis

> In healthcare-associated pneumonia with sepsis, piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g TDS IV provides broad-spectrum cover including Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which is an important consideration in patients with COPD from a care home setting. The absence of penicillin allergy, the healthcare-associated source, and the severity (sepsis criteria met) all support this choice. Antibiotic choice should always be guided by local antimicrobial policy and allergy status; this stem specifies no known allergies. Renal function monitoring is essential given potential nephrotoxicity, particularly alongside the haemodynamic compromise.

### Q3 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Cardiology

A 56-year-old female presents to the A&E with severe central chest pain radiating to the left arm, lasting 45 minutes. She has a history of hypertension and hyperlipidaemia. An ECG confirms ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).

**Complete a prescription for the most appropriate immediate treatment.**

DRUG: Aspirin | Acetylsalicylic acid
DOSE: 300 mg | 300mg | 0.3 g
ROUTE: Oral (PO) | PO | By mouth
FREQUENCY: Once only (STAT) | STAT | Single dose
INDICATION: Acute myocardial infarction

> Aspirin is the cornerstone of antiplatelet therapy in acute MI, reducing mortality by preventing further thrombus formation. The oral route is rapid and effective. A 300 mg loading dose is crucial for immediate platelet inhibition. Alternatives like clopidogrel are used adjunctively but not as a replacement for the initial aspirin treatment. Monitoring for bleeding is necessary.

### Q4 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Respiratory Medicine

A 67-year-old male known with COPD presents with increased sputum production, dyspnoea, and wheezing over the last 2 days. He has a respiratory rate of 24/min and an oxygen saturation of 88% on room air.

**Complete a prescription for the most appropriate immediate treatment.**

DRUG: Salbutamol | Ventolin
DOSE: 5 mg | 5000 micrograms | 5,000 mcg
ROUTE: Nebulised (NEB) | Neb | NEB
FREQUENCY: Immediately; repeat every 20-30 minutes as required | q20-30min PRN
INDICATION: Acute exacerbation of COPD

> In acute COPD exacerbations, nebulised salbutamol is first-line bronchodilator therapy and should be given promptly rather than prescribed as open-ended PRN. In COPD, the nebuliser should be air-driven (not oxygen-driven) and supplemental oxygen administered separately to a target SpO2 of 88-92%, avoiding excessive oxygen which can worsen hypercapnia. Ipratropium bromide 500 micrograms nebulised can be added for additional bronchodilation. Systemic corticosteroids (prednisolone 30-40 mg OD for 5 days) and antibiotics (if purulent sputum or signs of infection) are also required as part of the broader exacerbation management.

### Q5 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Endocrinology

A 23-year-old female with known type 1 diabetes mellitus presents with nausea, vomiting, and polyuria. Her blood glucose is 28 mmol/L, ketones are high, and venous blood gas shows a pH of 7.25.

**Complete a prescription for the most appropriate immediate treatment.**

DRUG: Insulin (Actrapid) | Soluble insulin
DOSE: 0.1 units/kg/hour | 0.1 U/kg/hr
ROUTE: Intravenous infusion (IV) | IV | Intravenously
FREQUENCY: Continuous infusion | Continuous | Infusion
INDICATION: Diabetic ketoacidosis

> In diabetic ketoacidosis, a fixed-rate intravenous insulin infusion (FRIII) at 0.1 units/kg/hour is the cornerstone of treatment to suppress ketogenesis and lower blood glucose. This question asks specifically for the insulin prescription. However, DKA management is not insulin alone: concurrent fluid resuscitation with 0.9% sodium chloride is the first priority and should be initiated immediately; potassium replacement is required from the outset given the risk of life-threatening hypokalaemia as insulin drives potassium intracellularly. Actrapid (soluble insulin) or novorapid can be used for FRIII. Subcutaneous insulin is inappropriate in DKA due to unreliable absorption in the context of dehydration and haemodynamic compromise.

### Q6 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Haematology

A 62-year-old male with a body weight of 80 kg presents with sudden onset of pleuritic chest pain and dyspnoea. A CT pulmonary angiogram confirms a pulmonary embolism.

**Complete a prescription for the most appropriate immediate treatment.**

DRUG: Enoxaparin | Clexane
DOSE: 80 mg | 1 mg/kg | 80mg
ROUTE: Subcutaneous (SC) | SC | Subcut
FREQUENCY: Every 12 hours (BD) | BD | Twice daily
INDICATION: Pulmonary embolism

> Enoxaparin, a low molecular weight heparin, is effective for initial anticoagulation in pulmonary embolism. The dosing is weight-based (1 mg/kg BD), allowing for predictable anticoagulant effects without routine monitoring. This is preferred over unfractionated heparin due to ease of administration and lower risk of HIT. Warfarin would be used for long-term anticoagulation but is not suitable for immediate anticoagulation.

### Q7 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Gastroenterology

A 47-year-old male presents with 5 episodes of diarrhoea daily for the past week. Clostridioides difficile toxin is detected in his stool sample. 

**Complete a prescription for the most appropriate immediate treatment.**

DRUG: Vancomycin | Vancocin
DOSE: 125 mg | 125mg | 0.125 g
ROUTE: Oral (PO) | PO | By mouth
FREQUENCY: Every 6 hours (QID) | QID | Four times daily
DURATION: 10 days
INDICATION: Clostridioides difficile infection

> Oral vancomycin 125 mg QDS for 10 days is first-line treatment for a first episode of non-severe Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) per NICE and PHE guidance. High intraluminal colonic concentrations are achieved with oral dosing; systemic absorption is negligible, so nephrotoxicity is not a significant concern. Fidaxomicin 200 mg BD for 10 days is an alternative (particularly for patients at high risk of recurrence) but is more costly. Oral metronidazole is no longer recommended as first-line for CDI. A full 10-day course should be completed to reduce the risk of treatment failure and recurrence.

### Q8 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Neurology

A 38-year-old female presents with a sudden, severe headache localised around the left eye, associated with tearing and nasal congestion. She has a history of cluster headaches.

**Complete a prescription for the most appropriate immediate treatment.**

DRUG: Sumatriptan | Imigran
DOSE: 6 mg | 6mg | 0.006 g
ROUTE: Subcutaneous (SC) | SC | Subcut
FREQUENCY: Once only (STAT) | STAT | Single dose
INDICATION: Acute cluster headache

> Sumatriptan is the treatment of choice for acute cluster headache attacks due to its rapid onset of action when administered subcutaneously. The 6 mg dose provides quick relief, often within 10-15 minutes. Alternative routes, like oral or nasal, are less effective due to slower absorption. Oxygen therapy can be adjunctively helpful but does not replace the efficacy of sumatriptan SC in aborting attacks.

A 68-year-old male with a history of chronic heart failure and hypertension presents with worsening ankle oedema and shortness of breath. He has been taking his prescribed medications consistently. On review, you notice his current prescription.

**Current prescription:**
| Drug | Dose | Route | Frequency | Indication |
|------|------|-------|-----------|------------|
| Ramipril | 10 mg | Oral | OD | Hypertension |
| Ibuprofen | 400 mg | Oral | TDS | Pain |

**Part A: What is the problem with this prescription?**

A. Incorrect dosing of ramipril
B. Risk of interaction between ibuprofen and ramipril ✓
C. Ibuprofen dose too high
D. Ramipril is contraindicated
E. Wrong indication for ibuprofen

**Part B: What action should you take?**

A. Discontinue ibuprofen ✓
B. Increase ramipril dose
C. Add furosemide
D. Replace ramipril with losartan
E. Reduce ibuprofen dose

### Q17 | MCQ | Planning Management | Cardiology

A 62-year-old female with a history of hypertension and type 2 diabetes presents with chest pain and is diagnosed with stable angina. Her current medications include ramipril, metformin, and atorvastatin. She was previously prescribed amlodipine but discontinued it due to ankle oedema. Her BP is 138/82 mmHg and her heart rate is 72 bpm.

**What is the most appropriate medication to add for the management of her stable angina?**

A. Amlodipine
B. Atenolol ✓
C. Ivabradine
D. Digoxin
E. Furosemide

> NICE guidelines (CG126) recommend offering either a beta-blocker or a calcium-channel blocker (CCB) as first-line treatment for stable angina; neither is universally superior. In this patient, ankle oedema with prior amlodipine makes a CCB less suitable, making a beta-blocker such as atenolol the most appropriate choice. Ivabradine is reserved for patients in whom both beta-blockers and CCBs are contraindicated or not tolerated, or in combination when inadequate control persists. Digoxin and furosemide are not indicated for stable angina.

### Q22 | MCQ | Planning Management | General Medicine

### Q25 | MCQ | Providing Prescribing Decisions | General Medicine

A 56-year-old female with a history of chronic kidney disease and hypertension is started on ramipril. She presents with a dry cough that she finds bothersome. Her current medications include amlodipine and atorvastatin. On examination, her blood pressure is 135/85 mmHg, and her renal function shows stable eGFR.

A. Stop ramipril immediately
B. Consider switching to an angiotensin II receptor blocker ✓
C. Increase the dose of ramipril
D. Add a cough suppressant
E. Continue with ramipril and reassess in 2 weeks

> ACE inhibitors like ramipril can cause a persistent dry cough as a side effect. According to NICE guidelines, if the cough is troublesome, switching to an angiotensin II receptor blocker (ARB) is recommended. Increasing the dose of ramipril or using a cough suppressant would not address the underlying cause. Stopping ramipril without an alternative may worsen hypertension control. Continuing ramipril without addressing the side effect is inappropriate.
