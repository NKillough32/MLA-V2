### Q1 | MCQ | Adverse Drug Reactions | Cardiology

A 58-year-old man was started on amiodarone 200 mg OD for persistent atrial fibrillation eight months ago. He now presents with a six-week history of progressive exertional dyspnoea and a dry cough. Chest X-ray shows bilateral interstitial infiltrates. Spirometry reveals a restrictive pattern.

**What is the most likely cause of his current respiratory symptoms?**

A. Acute decompensated heart failure
B. Pulmonary toxicity from amiodarone ✓
C. Community-acquired pneumonia
D. Pulmonary embolism
E. Pleural effusion secondary to amiodarone

> Amiodarone pulmonary toxicity occurs in approximately 5–10% of patients, typically after months to years of therapy. Features include progressive dyspnoea, dry cough, bilateral interstitial infiltrates on CXR, and a restrictive pattern on spirometry. Management: stop amiodarone, consider prednisolone 40–60 mg OD in severe cases. Recommended monitoring: annual CXR and PFTs; TFTs every 6 months; LFTs every 6 months; serum K⁺ and Mg²⁺ before starting.

### Q2 | MCQ | Drug Monitoring | Renal Medicine

A 72-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes and CKD stage 3b (eGFR 32 mL/min/1.73m²) is admitted with a urinary tract infection. Her regular medications include metformin 500 mg BD, ramipril 5 mg OD, and atorvastatin 20 mg OD. You are asked to review her medications.

**Which of her regular medications requires the most urgent review given her current clinical situation?**

A. Atorvastatin — requires dose reduction in CKD
B. Ramipril — must be stopped in all patients with CKD
C. Metformin — must be reviewed; consider dose reduction or temporary suspension given the risk of lactic acidosis ✓
D. All three are safe and require no change
E. None of the medications require review

> Metformin is generally safe in CKD stage 3b (eGFR 30–44) at a reduced dose, but during an acute illness (especially if the patient is dehydrated or at risk of AKI), metformin should be temporarily suspended — it accumulates in renal failure, increasing the risk of lactic acidosis. NICE guidance: use with caution at eGFR 30–45, stop if eGFR <30. During acute illness, apply sick day rules and temporarily stop. Atorvastatin does not require dose reduction in CKD. Ramipril is generally continued in CKD unless K⁺ is high or there is haemodynamic compromise.

### Q3 | CALCULATION | Calculation Skills | Paediatrics

A 16 kg child with status asthmaticus requires aminophylline as a loading dose. The recommended dose is 5 mg/kg IV over 20 minutes. The available preparation is aminophylline 250 mg/10 mL (25 mg/mL).

**Calculate the volume of aminophylline solution required for the loading dose, in mL.**

UNIT: mL
ANSWER: 3.2
TOLERANCE: 0.1
WORKING:
Step 1: Calculate dose = 16 kg × 5 mg/kg = 80 mg
Step 2: Calculate volume = 80 mg ÷ 25 mg/mL = 3.2 mL

> Aminophylline loading dose: 5 mg/kg IV over 20 min (omit loading dose if already on theophylline — check serum level first). Maintenance: approximately 0.5 mg/kg/hour. Therapeutic range: 10–20 mg/L. Toxicity (nausea, arrhythmias, seizures) occurs at levels >20 mg/L. Monitor serum levels and ECG during treatment.

### Q4 | CALCULATION | Calculation Skills | General Medicine

A patient requires a heparin infusion at 18 units/kg/hour. The patient weighs 80 kg. The available bag contains heparin 25,000 units in 50 mL 0.9% sodium chloride.

**Calculate the infusion rate in mL/hour.**

UNIT: mL/hour
ANSWER: 2.88
TOLERANCE: 0.05
WORKING:
Step 1: Hourly dose = 80 kg × 18 units/kg/hour = 1440 units/hour
Step 2: Concentration = 25,000 units ÷ 50 mL = 500 units/mL
Step 3: Rate = 1440 units/hour ÷ 500 units/mL = 2.88 mL/hour

> Heparin infusions require weight-based dosing monitored with APTT. Standard regimen: loading dose 80 units/kg IV, then 18 units/kg/hour infusion. Adjust based on APTT ratio (target 1.5–2.5 × control). Check APTT 6 hours after starting, then 6 hours after each dose change. Risk of heparin-induced thrombocytopaenia (HIT): check platelets every 2–3 days from day 4–14.

### Q5 | CALCULATION | Calculation Skills | Paediatrics

A 3-year-old child (weight 14 kg) requires oral ibuprofen for fever. The recommended dose is 5 mg/kg/dose. The available suspension is ibuprofen 100 mg/5 mL.

**Calculate the volume in mL to administer per dose.**

UNIT: mL
ANSWER: 3.5
TOLERANCE: 0.1
WORKING:
Step 1: Dose = 14 kg × 5 mg/kg = 70 mg
Step 2: Concentration = 100 mg / 5 mL = 20 mg/mL
Step 3: Volume = 70 mg ÷ 20 mg/mL = 3.5 mL

> Ibuprofen paediatric dosing: 5 mg/kg/dose (max 10 mg/kg/dose, absolute max 400 mg/dose) every 6–8 hours. Maximum daily dose: 30 mg/kg/day. Avoid in asthmatics sensitive to NSAIDs, children under 3 months, and dehydrated patients (AKI risk). Paracetamol can be alternated with ibuprofen for better fever control.

### Q6 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Emergency

A 28-year-old woman (weight 68 kg) is brought to the Emergency Department following a bee sting. She has developed generalised urticaria, audible stridor, and her blood pressure is 72/40 mmHg. She has no known drug allergies.

**Complete a prescription for the most appropriate immediate treatment.**

DRUG: Adrenaline (epinephrine) | Adrenaline | Epinephrine | Adrenaline (Epinephrine)
DOSE: 500 micrograms | 0.5 mg | 500mcg
ROUTE: Intramuscular (IM) | IM | Intramuscular
FREQUENCY: Once (STAT) | STAT | Once | Immediately | Single dose

> IM adrenaline 500 micrograms (0.5 mL of 1:1000 adrenaline) into the anterolateral thigh is the first-line treatment for anaphylaxis. The IM route provides faster, more reliable absorption than SC. Can be repeated every 5 minutes if no improvement. Simultaneously: high-flow O₂, IM chlorphenamine 10 mg, IV hydrocortisone 200 mg, IV fluid challenge (crystalloid 500 mL stat if hypotensive). Refer to resus guidelines (Resuscitation Council UK).

### Q7 | PRESCRIPTION | Prescription Writing | Infectious Disease

A 22-year-old woman presents with a 2-day history of dysuria, frequency, and suprapubic pain. She is otherwise well, apyrexial, and not pregnant. Urine dipstick: leucocytes +++, nitrites +, no blood. She has no known drug allergies.

**Complete a prescription for the recommended first-line antimicrobial treatment.**

DRUG: Nitrofurantoin | Nitrofurantoin monohydrate/macrocrystals | Nitrofurantoin MR
DOSE: 100 mg | 100mg
ROUTE: Oral | PO | By mouth
FREQUENCY: Twice daily (BD) | BD | Twice daily | Two times a day

> NICE guidance (NG109): uncomplicated lower UTI in women — first-line: nitrofurantoin 100 mg MR BD for 3 days (if eGFR ≥45). Second-line: trimethoprim 200 mg BD for 7 days (if low local resistance). Pivmecillinam 400 mg BD for 3 days is an alternative. Send MSU for culture before or at same time as starting antibiotics. Do not use nitrofurantoin if eGFR <45 (insufficient urinary concentration). Avoid trimethoprim in pregnancy (first trimester — folate antagonism).

### Q8 | MCQ | Calculation Skills | General Medicine

A pharmacist needs to prepare an IV infusion of potassium chloride. The order reads: "Add 20 mmol KCl to 1 litre of 0.9% NaCl and infuse over 4 hours." Available: potassium chloride 20 mmol/10 mL (2 mmol/mL) ampoules. What volume of potassium chloride concentrate should be added to the bag?

A. 5 mL
B. 10 mL ✓
C. 20 mL
D. 40 mL
E. 100 mL

> Volume = 20 mmol ÷ 2 mmol/mL = 10 mL. Maximum safe rate: 10 mmol/hour peripherally (20 mmol/hour via central line with continuous cardiac monitoring). Maximum peripheral concentration: 40 mmol/L. Never give undiluted KCl IV — cardiac arrest risk. Always dilute and use an infusion pump.

### Q9 | MCQ | Prescription Review | Pharmacology

A 45-year-old man is prescribed the following: Methotrexate 15 mg once weekly for rheumatoid arthritis. He attends his GP complaining of a productive cough and is prescribed co-trimoxazole (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) 960 mg BD for 7 days.

**What is the most serious potential consequence of this prescribing decision?**

A. Reduced efficacy of methotrexate due to drug interaction
B. Increased risk of hepatotoxicity from combined liver toxicity
C. Severe bone marrow suppression due to synergistic folate antagonism ✓
D. Nephrotoxicity from combined renal toxicity
E. Rash from hypersensitivity cross-reaction

> Methotrexate + trimethoprim is a NEVER-EVENT combination. Both drugs inhibit dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), causing synergistic folate depletion → severe pancytopenia and bone marrow failure, which can be fatal. Alternative antibiotics should always be used in patients on methotrexate: amoxicillin, doxycycline, or cefalexin are safe alternatives for respiratory infections. If trimethoprim is absolutely essential (e.g. Pneumocystis prophylaxis), close haematological monitoring is mandatory.

### Q10 | MCQ | Planning Management | Obstetrics

A 31-year-old woman with epilepsy (well-controlled on sodium valproate 1000 mg BD) attends pre-conception counselling. She is planning a pregnancy within the next year. She has focal-onset seizures and previously failed on lamotrigine monotherapy.

**What is the most important action regarding her current anticonvulsant medication?**

A. Continue valproate — the seizure risk of switching outweighs teratogenic risk
B. Switch immediately to phenytoin which is safer in pregnancy
C. Urgent referral to a specialist (neurologist/epilepsy midwife) to discuss risks and explore alternatives before conception — do not prescribe or continue valproate without specialist input ✓
D. Switch to lamotrigine immediately without specialist review
E. Stop anticonvulsant therapy entirely during the first trimester

> Sodium valproate carries a risk of major congenital malformations of approximately 10% and neurodevelopmental delay in up to 30–40% of children exposed in utero. The MHRA Valproate Pregnancy Prevention Programme (PPP) requires: pregnancy prevention programme for all women of childbearing potential; annual specialist review and signed acknowledgement of risks; two forms of effective contraception. Specialist review BEFORE conception is mandatory. Never switch anticonvulsants without specialist guidance — abrupt changes can trigger status epilepticus.

### Q11 | REVIEW | Prescription Review | General Medicine

MARKS_A: 2
MARKS_B: 2

A 67-year-old man with atrial fibrillation is admitted for an elective joint replacement. His regular medications are reviewed on the ward. He takes warfarin 5 mg OD (target INR 2–3) and has a new prescription written by the anaesthetic team for co-trimoxazole 960 mg BD as surgical prophylaxis. His most recent INR, taken 5 days ago, was 2.5. He has no known drug allergies.

**Current prescription:**
| Drug | Dose | Route | Frequency | Indication |
|------|------|-------|-----------|------------|
| Warfarin | 5 mg | Oral | Once daily | Atrial fibrillation |
| Co-trimoxazole | 960 mg | Oral | Twice daily | Surgical prophylaxis |

**Part A: Which of the following most accurately describes the drug interaction concern with this prescription?**

A. Co-trimoxazole induces CYP2C9, reducing warfarin levels and increasing the risk of thromboembolism
B. Co-trimoxazole inhibits CYP2C9 and reduces renal elimination of warfarin, significantly raising INR and bleeding risk ✓
C. Co-trimoxazole and warfarin compete for albumin binding, leading to warfarin toxicity via displacement alone
D. The interaction is minor and requires no monitoring beyond routine INR checks at 3 months
E. Warfarin is absolutely contraindicated in patients receiving any antibiotic

**Part B: What is the most appropriate action to take?**

A. Continue both drugs and recheck INR in 4 weeks at the patient's next clinic visit
B. Stop warfarin immediately and do not restart until the antibiotic course has finished
C. Stop co-trimoxazole, prescribe a safer alternative antibiotic, and arrange an urgent INR check within 3–5 days ✓
D. Halve the warfarin dose and continue co-trimoxazole with daily INR monitoring
E. Switch warfarin to a DOAC (e.g. apixaban) and continue co-trimoxazole as prescribed

> Co-trimoxazole inhibits CYP2C9 (major metabolic pathway for warfarin) and reduces renal excretion of the active S-warfarin enantiomer. This combination predictably and significantly raises INR — often to supratherapeutic levels — and greatly increases bleeding risk. **Action:** stop co-trimoxazole; prescribe a safe alternative (e.g. cefalexin or co-amoxiclav for surgical prophylaxis, depending on local policy); check INR within 3–5 days. If INR >5 at recheck: withold warfarin and consider oral vitamin K 1–3 mg. Switching to a DOAC is not appropriate in an acute perioperative setting without specialist input.

### Q12 | REVIEW | Prescription Review | Renal Medicine

MARKS_A: 2
MARKS_B: 2

A 78-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes, CKD stage 3b (eGFR 34 mL/min/1.73m²), and hypertension presents with an acute exacerbation of COPD and is started on oral prednisolone 40 mg OD. Her regular medications are shown below. She is not acutely unwell and does not require IV fluids.

**Current regular medications:**
| Drug | Dose | Route | Frequency | Indication |
|------|------|-------|-----------|------------|
| Metformin | 1000 mg | Oral | Twice daily | Type 2 diabetes |
| Ramipril | 5 mg | Oral | Once daily | Hypertension / CKD |
| Atorvastatin | 40 mg | Oral | Once daily at night | Cardiovascular risk |
| Prednisolone | 40 mg | Oral | Once daily | COPD exacerbation |

**Part A: Which of her medications requires the most urgent review in the context of her new prescription for prednisolone?**

A. Atorvastatin — prednisolone reduces its bioavailability significantly
B. Ramipril — prednisolone causes hyperkalaemia when co-prescribed with ACE inhibitors
C. Metformin — prednisolone causes hyperglycaemia; blood glucose monitoring and possible metformin dose adjustment or insulin initiation are required ✓
D. Prednisolone itself — oral steroids are absolutely contraindicated in CKD stage 3b
E. No medication review is required as all drugs are safe to co-prescribe with prednisolone

**Part B: Regarding her metformin, which of the following statements is correct?**

A. Metformin must be stopped immediately in all patients with CKD regardless of eGFR
B. Metformin is safe to continue at 1000 mg BD in CKD stage 3b without dose adjustment or any additional monitoring
C. Metformin dose should be reduced (maximum 500 mg BD) in CKD stage 3b; prednisolone-induced hyperglycaemia may necessitate insulin and temporary metformin suspension ✓
D. Metformin should be replaced with a sulfonylurea (e.g. gliclazide) in all patients with eGFR 30–44
E. Metformin is safe at full dose in CKD 3b; only the ramipril needs to be reviewed

> Prednisolone causes dose-dependent hyperglycaemia, often raising blood glucose post-prandially. In a patient already on metformin for type 2 diabetes with CKD 3b, this requires close monitoring of blood glucose and may necessitate additional antidiabetic therapy (often short-acting insulin). **Metformin in CKD 3b (eGFR 30–44):** NICE guidance recommends dose reduction to maximum 500 mg BD (or 1 g daily) with caution; it should be temporarily suspended if eGFR falls below 30 (e.g. during AKI). Lactic acidosis risk increases if metformin accumulates in renal failure, particularly if the patient becomes dehydrated or acutely unwell. Ramipril is appropriate in CKD for renoprotection; monitor K⁺ and eGFR. Atorvastatin does not interact significantly with prednisolone at standard doses.
