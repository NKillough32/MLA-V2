### Q1 | PRESCRIBING | Prescribing | Cardiology

DRUG_MARKS: 5
DOSE_MARKS: 5

**Case presentation**
A 74-year-old man presents to the medical assessment unit with diarrhoea, vomiting and reduced oral intake. **PMH.** Hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, benign prostatic hypertrophy. **DH.** Ramipril 10 mg PO daily, amlodipine 10 mg PO daily, indapamide 2.5 mg PO daily, amiloride hydrochloride 5 mg PO daily, tamsulosin hydrochloride m/r 400 micrograms PO daily, atorvastatin 40 mg PO nightly.

**On examination**
Temperature 37.1°C, HR 109/min and rhythm regular, BP 104/69 mmHg, JVP not seen, RR 17/min, O_2 sat 95% breathing air. HS normal, chest sounds clear.

**Investigations**
Na^+ 148 mmol/L (137–144), K^+ 7.2 mmol/L (3.5–5.3), U 24.9 mmol/L (2.5–7.0), Cr 253 µmol/L (60–110), eGFR 23 mL/min/1.73 m^2 (>60).
ECG is as shown (see image).

An infusion of sodium chloride 0.9% 500 mL over 10 minutes is started.

**Prescribing request**
Write a prescription for ONE drug that is *most appropriate* for the next step of management in this patient.
(*use the ‘once-only medicines' prescription form *provided)

**Drug choice**
> The BNF states ' Hyperkalaemia in the presence of ECG changes calls for urgent treatment with intravenous calcium chloride 10% [unlicensed use] or calcium gluconate 10%, to temporarily protect against myocardial excitability. Calcium gluconate is the preferred calcium salt, except in a cardiac arrest/peri-arrest situation where calcium chloride is preferred.' Insulin would be used second line to lower the serum potassium, but its onset of effect is slow. Though beta-2 agonists are occasionally used to lower serum potassium, their effects are unreliable and offset by other adverse effects, such as tachycardia. Cation-exchange polymers (patiromer calcium and sodium zirconium cyclosilicate) are also part of the treatment pathway but will not provide immediate cardiac stabilisation that is required.

**Dose / route / frequency**
> The correct dose of calcium gluconate is 30 mL of a 10% solution (3 g). An MHRA review of UK data in the context of severe hyperkalaemia and cardiac arrest has identified reports of incidents, including fatalities, associated with incorrect calcium gluconate dosing, a lack of potassium-lowering treatment, and lack of, or inappropriate, ECG monitoring.

**Optimal answers**

DRUG_OPTION: calcium chloride dihydrate 100 mg/mL injection
  DOSE: 10 mL intravenous once only
  DOSE: 1 g intravenous once only

DRUG_OPTION: calcium gluconate 100 mg/mL injection
  DOSE: 30 mL intravenous once only
  DOSE: 3 g intravenous once only

**On examination**

**Investigations**
ECG shows atrial fibrillation.
Echocardiogram shows no valvular pathology.
CT scan head shows general atrophy, no signs of acute ischaemia or haemorrhage.

**Prescribing request**

(*use the ‘regular medicines’* prescription form provided)

**Drug choice**
> Drug Choice: The prescribing request is for one drug that is most appropriate for reducing the risk of the patients symptoms reccuring. The symptoms are indicative of transient ischaemic stroke and prophylaxis will require anticoagulation. The BNF treatment summary for 'oral anticoagulants' outlines drug options available. Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs) are used for the prevention of stroke and systemic embolism prophylaxis of stroke and systemic embolism in non-valvular atrial fibrillation and at least one risk factor (such as previous stroke or transient ischaemic attack, symptomatic heart failure, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, or age 75 years and over). Apixaban, edoxaban and rivaroxaban are reversible inhibitors of activated factor X (factor Xa) which prevents thrombin generation and thrombus development. Dose, route and frequency of administration: You must take into account the patient demographics and any cautions particularly with weight and renal function regarding dosing for DOACs. The BNF monographs will specify dose and frequencies appropriate to the patient. Further information: All patients with atrial fibrillation should be assessed for their risk of stroke and thromboembolism. Prior to and during anticoagulation treatment, assess stroke risk using the CHA 2 DS 2 -VASc risk tool and bleeding risk using the ORBIT bleeding risk tool.

### Q4 | PRESCRIBING | Prescribing | Respiratory

DRUG_MARKS: 5
DOSE_MARKS: 5

**Case presentation**
A 74-year-old man on the elderly care ward is being treated for an acute exacerbation of COPD. He reports a sore mouth. **PMH.** COPD, AF, hypertension. **DH.** Beclometasone dipropionate 87 micrograms/formoterol 5 micrograms/glycopyrronium 9 micrograms/dose (Trimbow^®) two puffs INH 12-hrly, amlodipine 5 mg PO daily, warfarin PO as per INR, amoxicillin 1 g PO 8-hrly.

**On examination**
Temperature 36.9°C, HR 80/min and rhythm irregular, BP 140/86 mmHg, RR 20/min. White furry plaques are evident on his tongue and palate.

**Prescribing request**
Write a prescription for ONE drug that is *most appropriate* to treat the cause of his sore mouth.
(*use the ‘regular medicines’* *prescription form *provided)

**Drug choice**
> Drug Choice: This is guided by the BNF treament summary 'oropharyngeal fungal infections'. The 'Antifungals, system use' summary is not helpful in this case. For mild to moderate localised infections miconazole is cited as first line, but you must be cautious of interactions due to systemic absorption. In this case the patient is also taking warfarin and therefore, miconazole is a less prefered option as close INR monitoring would be required. Nystatin would be the preferred option, as is not absorbed from the gastro-intestinal tract. Nystatin is applied locally (as a suspension) to the mouth for treating local fungal infections. The route of administration should be oromucosal or buccal, rather than oral (PO), as the patient will not 'swallow' the medication in order for it to have effect. Nystatin requires contact time on the palate and within the oropharynx.

**Dose / route / frequency**
> The route is incorrect - 'Oral (PO)' implies that it should be swallowed, whereas it should be prescribed for 'oromucosal' or 'buccal' administration, implying that it is held in the mouth.

**Optimal answers**

DRUG_OPTION: nystatin 100 000 units/mL oral suspension
  DOSE: 1 mL oromucosal four times daily
  DOSE: 1 mL buccal four times daily
  DOSE: 100 000 units oromucosal four times daily
  DOSE: 100 000 units buccal four times daily

**Case presentation**
An 80-year-old woman presents to her GP with fatigue. **PMH.** Coeliac disease, irritable bowel syndrome. **DH. **Omeprazole 20 mg PO daily, peppermint oil one capsule PO daily. **SH.** Lives alone, walks with a stick.

**On examination**
Conjunctival pallor. Abdomen mildly distended, bowel sounds present.

**Investigations**
Hb 90 g/L (115–165), MCV 100 fL (80–96), serum vitamin B_12 300 ng/L (160–760), serum folate 1.0 μg/L (2.0–11.0), serum ferritin 123 micrograms/L (15–300).

**Prescribing request**
Write a prescription for ONE drug that is *most appropriate* to treat her anaemia.
(*use the 'general practice' prescription form provided*)

**Drug choice**
> Drug Choice: The BNF treatment summary 'Anaemia, megaloblastic' outlines how to identify and manage folate deficiency. This patient has correctly had B12 and folate investigations carried out following symptom identification, which identify folate deficient megaloblastic anaemia. Dose, route and frequency of administration: The BNF recommends 5 mg of folic acid daily for 4 months in folate-deficient megaloblastic anemia. Folic acid is used in 10-15 mg for malabsorption states and is unlikely to cause harm, so scores for these are not heavily penalised Further information: Folate deficiency is common in coeliac disease but not typically associated with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). Due to malabsorption from damage to the small intestine’s lining, individuals with coeliac disease often have difficulty absorbing nutrients, including folate. Folate deficiency is thus a common complication in untreated or poorly managed celiac disease, leading to issues like megaloblastic anemia. IBS is a functional gastrointestinal disorder, meaning it affects bowel function but does not particularly cause structural damage to the intestines. Since there is no impaired absorption in IBS, folate deficiency is uncommon in this condition.

**Dose / route / frequency**
> The BNF recommends 5 mg of folic acid daily for 4 months in folate-deficient megaloblastic anaemia.

  DOSE: 1 tablet oral daily

Write a prescription for ONE drug that is *most appropriate* at this stage.
(*use the 'general practice' prescription form provided*)

In women with a uterus a progestogen needs to be added to long-term oestrogen therapy for hormone replacement, to prevent cystic hyperplasia of the endometrium and possible transformation to cancer; it can be added on a cyclical or a continuous basis." Cyclical treatments should be used if women are still menstruating, and for several months after menstruation has ceased. A product containing oestrogen and progestogen, that releases the same dose continuously, rather than a sequential product, will avoid withdrawal bleeding. The patient's preference is for a transdermal patch, rather than a tablet formulation. As the patient's last menstrual period is now more than 2 months ago, she can be switched to a continuous patch preparation.

**Dose / route / frequency**
> This is a continuous preparation of HRT, which would resolve the withdrawal bleeds, and matches the patient's preference for a transdermal patch, rather than a tablet formulation.
